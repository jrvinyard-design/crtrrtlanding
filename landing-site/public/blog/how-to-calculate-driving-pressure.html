How to Calculate Driving Pressure (And Why It's on the TMC)

Driving pressure shows up constantly in ARDS ventilator management questions on the TMC, and it's one of those calculations that's simple to compute but easy to misunderstand. Here's exactly how it works, why it matters clinically, and how the exam likes to test it.

The formula

Driving Pressure = Plateau Pressure − PEEP

Sometimes written as ΔP = Pplat − PEEP

That's it — it's a subtraction. The part students actually get tripped up on is which pressure to use (plateau, not peak) and what the number actually represents once you have it.

Why plateau pressure, not peak pressure

Peak pressure reflects both airway resistance and lung compliance combined — it's affected by how fast gas is flowing through the airways, not just how stretchy the lung tissue itself is. Plateau pressure, measured during a brief pause with no gas flow, isolates just the compliance side of the equation.

Driving pressure is specifically trying to capture how much pressure change was needed to deliver the tidal volume, accounting for lung and chest wall stretchiness — so it needs plateau pressure, not peak, to be meaningful.

What the number actually tells you

Driving pressure reflects tidal volume relative to the patient's respiratory system compliance. In plain terms: it's telling you how "stiff" the lungs are, adjusted for how big a breath you're actually giving.

  • A high driving pressure suggests the lungs are stiff relative to the tidal volume being delivered — a stretched, less compliant lung is having to accommodate more pressure change than it should for that volume.
  • A driving pressure consistently above roughly 15 cmH2O in ARDS patients has been associated with worse outcomes in research on lung-protective ventilation, which is part of why it's become a parameter some clinicians watch alongside — not instead of — plateau pressure and tidal volume targets.

Worked example

Given: A patient on volume control ventilation has a plateau pressure of 26 cmH2O and PEEP set at 8 cmH2O.

Calculate: Driving pressure = 26 − 8 = 18 cmH2O

This is on the higher end — worth investigating whether tidal volume can be reduced further (within lung-protective limits) or whether PEEP is optimally titrated for this patient's compliance.

How the TMC likes to test this

You won't usually just get asked "what's the formula." More often, the exam gives you a clinical scenario — ventilator settings, a patient history, sometimes a trending set of values over time — and asks you to either calculate the driving pressure yourself, or interpret what a given driving pressure means for the care plan (should PEEP change? Should tidal volume change? Neither?).

That's exactly why memorizing the formula alone isn't enough — you also need to practice applying it inside realistic scenarios, which is a different skill than recall.
